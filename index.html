Start With the Business

Three questions your campaign reports can't answer

Most leadership teams can quote last month's ROAS and cost per lead. Far fewer can answer these:

  1. Is our marketing investment the right size for the growth we want?
  2. Did the extra money we put into marketing last year actually pay?
  3. What can we afford to spend to win one new customer?

These are business questions, not campaign questions — and the answers are sitting in your P&L and your customer data. This guide works through each one, with two calculators so you can run your own numbers as you go.

The first number I look at

When I assess a company's marketing, I don't open the ad accounts. I take two numbers from the P&L — total revenue and total marketing investment — and divide.

ROM=Total Revenue÷Total Marketing Investment
A $5M company investing $500K in marketing runs at 10:1 — $10 of revenue for every $1 of marketing.

"Total" means total: paid media, marketing salaries, agency fees, creative, software. Count only ad spend and the ratio flatters you.

Return on Marketing is an efficiency ratio, not true ROI: not all of that revenue came from this year's marketing, and revenue isn't profit. So the ratio doesn't answer anything by itself. It starts the right conversation — is that level of investment right for this business?

Questions 1 & 2 · Is It the Right Size? Did It Pay?

Two numbers decide it: the growth you want and the profit you keep

Marketing exists to produce growth, so start there. How much do you want sales to grow next year? For an established business, that one figure puts you in one of three postures, each with a typical level of investment. The ranges come from the benchmarks in How Much Should You Spend on Marketing?

Under 5% growth
Maintenance
Marketing at 3–5% of sales. Not optional — it's roughly what it takes to stay relevant and hold your position.
5–15% growth
Healthy growth
Marketing at 6–8% of sales. Steady, compounding growth ahead of the market.
15%+ growth
Aggressive growth
Marketing at 8–12% of sales. Taking share, entering new segments, outrunning competitors.

These apply to established businesses. For a startup or a new market, all bets are off: you'll often invest 12–20% of sales or more, frequently at a loss, to get the flywheel turning. That can be exactly right — as long as it's a decision and not an accident.

Then check the goal against your P&L. Sales come in, the cost of delivering comes out, overhead comes out, marketing comes out — and what's left is yours. Flip that around:

What the P&L can fund=Gross Profit−Overhead−Profit You Want to Keep

How firmly to hold that line depends on who is paying for the growth. A bootstrapped business — growing out of its own profits — should treat it as a real limit, because every marketing dollar comes out of what the owners would otherwise keep. With outside capital, you can choose to run past it for a while.

What the P&L Can't Tell You

Growth is often paid for with this year's profit.

When a business pushes marketing up to grow faster, sales usually respond — and profit often dips, because new customers often don't pay back in their first year. That isn't failure. It's a trade: this year's margin for customers who keep buying.

Whether it's a good trade is something the P&L can't tell you. It depends on what those customers are worth after the first sale.

Question 3 · What Can You Afford to Pay for a Customer?

The first transaction isn't the whole relationship

The most expensive mistake I see isn't overspending on acquisition. It's judging acquisition against the first sale — and walking away from customers who would have been worth winning.

Four questions change the math: How often do they come back? How much do they spend? What does it cost to serve them? How long do they stay? And one discipline: measure lifetime value in contribution, not revenue. Every repeat purchase still costs something to deliver.

Home services: a first appointment that barely pays

In a home services business, acquiring a customer can cost 30–40% of the revenue from their first appointment. Add the cost of delivering the service and the first job barely breaks even. But many customers come back about once a year, and a typical relationship lasts around five years.

Same customer, two ways of looking at it

Same customer, same $105. On the first view, marketing is a cost center. On the second, it buys $105 a year in contribution for a one-time $105. That's the difference between cutting the program and scaling it.

eCommerce: repeat purchasing at Zenni Optical

At Zenni, repeat behavior was central to how we evaluated acquisition. Customers typically came back for more eyewear, on average about once every two years. Knowing the order value and the repeat cycle let us value a customer beyond the first order — and made acquisition investments defensible that looked marginal against a single sale.

The strategic implication

When you know what a customer is worth over time, you decide how aggressively to invest — instead of letting a first-order ROAS target decide for you. Companies that know this number can outbid competitors who don't, and still make money.

Cumulative contribution vs. acquisition cost

Bars show contribution earned by the end of each year. The gold line is what you paid to acquire the customer.

How this is calculated (and what it leaves out)
  • Standard mode: expected purchases = purchases per year × years (minimum one — the first transaction). Revenue = purchases × transaction value.
  • Retention mode: year-one purchases equal the yearly rate; each later year is multiplied by retention again: purchases = rate × (1 + r + r² + … + r^(years−1)), minimum one. The horizon caps the projection; retention does the decaying.
  • Contribution = revenue × contribution margin. Use the margin left after delivery and volume-driven costs, not gross margin — otherwise results will look better than reality. It's the money left to cover acquisition, fixed overhead, and profit, not net profit.
  • Contribution after CAC = lifetime contribution − CAC. LTV : CAC uses contribution, not revenue.
  • Payback is when cumulative contribution first covers CAC, assuming purchases are spread evenly through each year.
  • Not included: discounting future cash, price changes, referrals, rising service costs, or overhead. Treat results as directional estimates, not forecasts or guarantees.
Where Campaign Metrics Fit

So where does ROAS belong?

You've now looked at marketing from the business level and the customer level. Most reporting lives at a third: the channel.

ROAS=Ad-Attributed Revenue÷Ad Spend

It's a valuable number for the people running media. But campaigns do different jobs, and judging them all by ROAS quietly starves the programs that create next quarter's demand.

An awareness campaign should be judged on qualified reach. A traffic campaign on relevant visits and what people do after the click — a high click-through rate shows interest in the ad, not that the visitor is qualified or profitable. A conversion campaign on acquisition cost. A retention program on repeat purchases. The measure follows the job.

Align Investment With Ambition

When to lean in — and when to fix first

Put the P&L view and the customer view together, and the decision gets clearer. Most businesses land in one of two positions.

Can lean in
Higher acquisition costs are justified
  • Strong contribution margins
  • Customers who reliably come back
  • Capacity to deliver more without breaking service
  • Cash flow that can carry the payback period
Fix first
Improve the economics before scaling spend
  • Thin margins after delivery costs
  • Weak repeat behavior or high churn
  • Operations already at or near capacity
  • Payback longer than the business can finance

Capacity gets overlooked most. I've watched businesses buy demand they couldn't serve well — and the poor experiences cost them the repeat customers that made the math work in the first place.

The goal is not the highest possible ROAS. It's an acquisition strategy the business can sustain, sized to the growth you're actually trying to achieve.

Before Your Next Budget Decision

Five questions every business owner should answer

If you ran both calculators, you already have rough answers to most of these. Write them down — they're the agenda for your next budget conversation.

  1. What is our total marketing investment, and how does it compare with revenue?

    Count everything, not just ad spend. It's the baseline for every decision after it.

  2. What does a typical customer spend with us over the next five years?

    If you don't know, your acquisition targets are being set by the first sale by default. Pull repeat behavior from your customer data.

  3. How much contribution profit does that customer generate?

    Revenue flatters. Contribution is what's actually available to pay for acquisition, overhead, and profit.

  4. How much can we sustainably invest to acquire a customer?

    Your ceiling on acquisition cost — set by contribution, payback time, and cash. Know it and you can bid with confidence.

  5. Is our investment aligned with our growth goal and our capacity?

    An aggressive goal on a cautious budget, or an aggressive budget the operation can't absorb — either way, one has to change before the money is spent.

Better growth decisions come from the economics of the whole customer relationship — not individual transactions or campaigns.

Campaign data tells you what happened. Customer economics tells you what you can afford to make happen next.
