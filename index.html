Start With the Business

Three questions your campaign reports can't answer

Most leadership teams can quote last month's ROAS and cost per lead. Far fewer can answer these:

  1. Is our marketing investment the right size for the growth we want?
  2. Did the extra money we put into marketing last year actually pay?
  3. What can we afford to spend to win one new customer?

The answers sit in your P&L and your customer data. Two calculators below let you run your own numbers.

The first number I look at

I don't start in the ad accounts. I take two numbers from the P&L and divide.

ROM=Total Revenue÷Total Marketing Investment
A $5M company investing $500K in marketing runs at 10:1: $10 of revenue for every $1 of marketing.

Count everything: media, salaries, agencies, creative, software.

Return on Marketing is an efficiency ratio, not ROI, and revenue isn't profit. But it starts the right conversation: is that level of investment right for this business?

Questions 1 & 2 · Is It the Right Size? Did It Pay?

Two numbers decide it: the growth you want and the profit you keep

How Much Should You Spend on Marketing? showed that budget should follow ambition. This is the next step: checking that ambition against your own P&L. Start with one figure: how much do you want sales to grow next year?

These apply to established businesses. A startup or new market often takes 12–20% or more, frequently at a loss, to get the flywheel turning.

Then check it against the P&L. What's left after the cost of delivering and overhead has to cover both marketing and profit:

What the P&L can fund=Gross Profit−Overhead−Profit You Want to Keep

A bootstrapped business should treat that as a real limit. With outside capital, you can choose to run past it for a while.

What the P&L Can't Tell You

Growth is often paid for with this year's profit.

Push marketing up and sales usually respond, while profit dips: the next dollar rarely works as hard as the last, and new customers often don't pay back in year one. Whether that's a good trade depends on what those customers are worth after the first sale.

Question 3 · What Can You Afford to Pay for a Customer?

The first transaction isn't the whole relationship

The most expensive mistake I see isn't overspending on acquisition. It's judging acquisition against the first sale, and walking away from customers who were worth winning.

What matters is how often customers come back, what they spend, and what it costs to serve them. Measure it in contribution, not revenue: every repeat purchase still costs something to deliver.

Home services: a first job that barely pays

In home services, acquiring a customer often costs a third to half of the revenue from their first job.1 After the cost of delivering it, the first job barely pays. What changes the math is how many customers come back: 10–15% for a typical operator, 60% or more for the best.

eCommerce: repeat purchasing at Zenni Optical

At Zenni, customers typically came back for more eyewear about once every two years. Knowing the order value and that repeat cycle made acquisition investments defensible that looked marginal against a single sale.

Worth it, and can you afford to wait?

Lifetime value tells you whether a customer is worth buying. Payback tells you whether you can afford to wait. Recovering the cost in 30 days and recovering it in two years are very different businesses, and if you fund growth from profits, payback is often the real limit.

Companies that know these numbers can outbid competitors who don't, and still make money.

Cumulative contribution vs. acquisition cost

Bars: contribution earned by the end of each year. Gold line: what you paid to acquire the customer.

How this is calculated
  • Purchases = purchases per year × years, minimum one. With retention on, each later year is multiplied by the retention rate.
  • Contribution = revenue × contribution margin. It pays for acquisition, fixed overhead, and profit; it isn't net profit.
  • Payback is when cumulative contribution first covers CAC. Under about 12 months reads as quick, over about 24 as slow.
  • Not included: discounting, price changes, referrals, overhead. These are directional estimates, not forecasts.
Where Campaign Metrics Fit

So where does ROAS belong?

ROAS=Ad-Attributed Revenue÷Ad Spend

ROAS is the right number for the people running media. But campaigns do different jobs: awareness is judged on qualified reach, traffic on relevant visits, conversion on acquisition cost, retention on repeat purchases. And a high click-through rate shows interest in the ad, not that the visitor is profitable.

LevelMetricAnswersBlind spot
BusinessROMIs total marketing in proportion to the business?Ignores margin and what drove the revenue
ChannelROASIs this campaign doing its job?Ignores what happens after the first sale
CustomerLTVWhat is a customer worth, and what can we pay for one?Built on assumptions about the future

Campaign managers optimize channels. Business leaders decide whether the overall investment supports profitable growth. The goal is not the highest possible ROAS. It's an acquisition strategy the business can sustain.

Before Your Next Budget Decision

Five questions every business owner should answer

  1. What is our total marketing investment, and how does it compare with revenue?

    Count everything, not just ads. It's the baseline for every other decision.

  2. What does a typical customer spend with us over the next five years?

    Without it, the first sale sets your acquisition targets by default.

  3. How much contribution profit does that customer generate?

    Revenue flatters. Contribution is what pays for acquisition, overhead, and profit.

  4. How much can we sustainably invest to acquire a customer?

    Your ceiling, set by contribution, payback, and cash.

  5. Is our investment aligned with our growth goal and our capacity?

    If the goal and the budget don't match, or operations can't absorb the growth, one has to change first.

Better growth decisions come from the economics of the whole customer relationship, not individual transactions or campaigns.
Where the Real Work Is

The model is the easy part

These calculators show you how to think about the decision. They can't make it, because they average over what matters most:

  • Which channels still have room
  • How fast returns fall as spend scales
  • Which customers come back
  • How much overhead really grows with sales

Two businesses with the same P&L can need opposite decisions. That's where a growth plan is won or lost, and it's the work I do with clients.
